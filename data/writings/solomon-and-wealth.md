In the Bible's telling, it's a rise-and-fall story.

The wealth started as a bonus. In 1 Kings 3, Solomon asks God for wisdom to govern well instead of riches, and God gives him riches and honor as well. For a while the story treats the money as a sign of blessing, and 1 Kings 10 reads like a list of his achievements.

He spent lavishly. The inside of the Temple was covered in gold, down to the floor, and his own palace took 13 years to build, nearly twice as long as the Temple. He made 200 large shields of hammered gold at roughly 15 pounds each, 300 smaller ones, and an ivory throne covered in gold with twelve lions lining its six steps. All his drinking cups were gold, since silver was "nothing accounted of" in his day, and the text says he made silver as common as stones in Jerusalem. He also built up 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horses, importing horses from Egypt and even reselling chariots to neighboring kings.

Keeping all that going was hard on ordinary people. Twelve districts took turns supplying the royal court for a month each, and one day's food included 30 cattle and 100 sheep. The text also lists tens of thousands of conscripted laborers, including 30,000 Israelites sent to Lebanon in rotating shifts to cut timber.

The bigger problem was spiritual. Deuteronomy 17 warns Israel's kings not to pile up horses (especially from Egypt), wives, or silver and gold. Solomon did all three, and 1 Kings 10–11 covers them one after another. He had 700 wives and 300 concubines, many of them from political alliances, and in his old age they led him to build shrines to other gods like Chemosh and Molech. God tells him the kingdom will be torn from his family after he dies. The parallel account in 2 Chronicles leaves this part out and focuses on his splendor and the Temple.

The consequences came quickly. After Solomon died, the people asked his son Rehoboam to lighten the "heavy yoke" his father had put on them. Rehoboam threatened to make it heavier, so the ten northern tribes broke away and stoned the official in charge of forced labor. About five years later, Shishak of Egypt raided Jerusalem and carried off the Temple and palace treasures, including every one of those gold shields. Rehoboam replaced them with bronze ones.

Ecclesiastes, traditionally attributed to Solomon, reads like a look back on all of it. The writer describes gathering silver and gold and denying himself nothing, then concludes it was "all vanity and vexation of spirit."
